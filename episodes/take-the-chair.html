Three takeaways for first-time chiefs of staff

The chief of staff is a force multiplier — producer and tour manager for the CEO, extending the principal's reach without ever stealing their voice. In this first episode, Marcus hands over the starter kit he wishes someone had given him on day one: the three lessons everything else in the role builds on.

What you'll hear

  • Multiply the principal — learn how they think, protect their time and attention, and never try to be them
  • Run the operating system — the calendar, the priorities, the weekly leadership meeting, the written decision log
  • Follow-through as a discipline: what gets decided gets done, visibly
  • Build trust the only way it lasts — discretion about what you hear, candor about what you think
  • Private candor, public loyalty: the egoless force multiplier

Full transcript

Maya: Welcome to the Chief of Staff Playbook, the show where we help chiefs of staff get better at the job, one episode at a time. I'm Maya, and I'm here with Marcus, who has worked alongside executives across tech, healthcare, and finance as a chief of staff and advisor. Marcus, thanks for being here.

Marcus: Thanks for having me, Maya. Great topic to kick off with.

Maya: So today we're talking to first time chiefs of staff, or anyone who remembers being one. You've got three takeaways you wish someone had handed you on day one. But first, a quick level set, because the title means different things at different companies. What does a chief of staff actually do.

Marcus: Great question, because the role is famously fuzzy. At its core, a chief of staff is a force multiplier for a senior leader, usually the C E O. You take the things that need the leader's judgment but not necessarily the leader's time, and you make them happen. If the C E O is the lead singer, you're the producer and the tour manager rolled into one. You run the operating system around them so they can spend their time where only they can add value.

Maya: Producer and tour manager. Okay, takeaway number one. What is it.

Marcus: Multiply the principal, don't try to be the principal.

Maya: That sounds obvious, but I bet it's where most new chiefs of staff go wrong.

Marcus: Exactly. You get hired because you're capable and driven and full of opinions. Then you start acting like a second C E O, making calls that aren't yours to make. Or you swing the other way and become a glorified assistant, just scheduling and taking notes. Both miss the point. Your job is to extend your principal's reach and judgment, not replace either one.

Maya: How do you know where that line is.

Marcus: Three moves. First, learn how your principal thinks. In your first thirty days, study them like it's your job, because it is. How do they make decisions. What do they care about most. What annoys them. What does a good week look like for them. The better you can predict their reaction, the more you can act on their behalf with confidence.

Maya: So you're building a mental model of the person.

Marcus: Exactly. Second, get ruthless about their time and attention. Audit the calendar. Which meetings actually need them. Which decisions actually need them. Your superpower is giving them back hours and focus. Every recurring meeting should earn its place. If it doesn't need the C E O, it doesn't get the C E O.

Maya: I imagine that ruffles feathers, telling people they can't have time with the boss.

Marcus: It does, which is why the third move matters. Communicate the why. You're not gatekeeping, you're protecting the company's most scarce resource, which is the leader's attention. When people understand that, most of them get on board. And the ones who don't, well, that's useful information too.

Maya: Okay, so takeaway one is multiply the principal, don't try to be the principal. Learn how they think, protect their time, and explain the why. What's number two.

Marcus: Run the operating system.

Maya: The meetings, the follow through, the unglamorous machinery.

Marcus: The unglamorous machinery is the job. Most executives don't fail for lack of vision. They fail because decisions don't get made, or get made twice, or get made and then nobody follows through. As chief of staff, you own the machine. The weekly leadership meeting, the decision log, the follow up.

Maya: Give me the concrete version. What does a good operating system look like.

Marcus: Three parts. First, a real weekly leadership meeting with a tight agenda. What matters this week, what decisions do we need, what's stuck. You run it. You keep it on time, you keep it honest, and you make sure it ends with clear owners and deadlines. If your staff meeting is a status parade where everyone reads slides, kill that format and rebuild it.

Maya: Status parade. I've sat through a few of those.

Marcus: Everyone has. Second, write decisions down. A simple decision log. What was decided, who decided it, when, and why. You would not believe how much executive time gets wasted re litigating decisions nobody wrote down. The log ends that.

Maya: That seems so small, but I can see how it compounds.

Marcus: It really does. Third, be the follow through machine. After every meeting, the notes go out within hours, with owners and dates. Then you check. Not in a nagging way, in a steady way. Did the thing happen. If not, what's in the way. Accountability without drama. That's the chief of staff's signature move.

Maya: So takeaway two is run the operating system. Tight meetings, written decisions, relentless follow through. What's the third.

Marcus: Build trust through discretion and candor.

Maya: Those two sound like opposites. Discretion is holding things close, candor is saying the hard thing.

Marcus: They're two sides of the same coin, and the combination is what makes a chief of staff invaluable. Discretion first. You will hear everything. Board politics, compensation, who's struggling, what's really going on. If you leak, even once, even something small, you're done. People need to know that what they tell you stays with you. That trust is the currency the whole role runs on.

Maya: And candor.

Marcus: Candor is the other half. The higher someone rises, the less honest feedback they get. Everyone filters. Your job is to be the person who doesn't filter. When your principal's big idea has a hole in it, you say so, privately and respectfully, before it goes public. When they're about to walk into a meeting unprepared for the real dynamic, you brief them on it.

Maya: That takes guts, telling your boss they're wrong.

Marcus: It does, and that's why most people won't do it, and why it's so valuable when you do. The key is private candor, public loyalty. Challenge in the room with just the two of you. Outside that room, you're aligned. Never surprise your principal, and never let anyone see daylight between you in public.

Maya: Private candor, public loyalty. I love that framing. Let me recap the three, and you tell me if I've got them right. One, multiply the principal, don't try to be the principal. Two, run the operating system. Three, build trust through discretion and candor.

Marcus: You nailed it.

Maya: If someone takes one thing from today, what should it be.

Marcus: The best chiefs of staff are egoless force multipliers. Your wins are invisible by design. When the leader shines and the organization hums, that's your work, even if nobody claps for it. If you need the credit, this is the wrong job. If you love the impact, there's nothing better.

Maya: Egoless force multiplier. Marcus, this was fantastic. What's coming tomorrow.

Marcus: Tomorrow we go deeper on the relationship with your principal. How to run a great weekly one on one with your C E O, and the five questions that surface problems before they become crises.

Maya: Can't wait. Thanks for listening to the Chief of Staff Playbook. If this helped you, share it with one person stepping into the role. We'll see you tomorrow.
