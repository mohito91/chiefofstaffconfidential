Protect Your Attention

Attention is the scarcest resource.

Attention is the scarcest resource in the building — not money, not talent. And the chief of staff is its guardian: yours, and your principal's. Every priority you accept spends it; every meeting you allow spends it. This episode is the system for protecting it: five or six real priorities and no more, the Now / Soon / Never sort, the one question that separates real urgency from transferred anxiety, and the weekly review that keeps the list honest — kill one thing every week.

What you'll hear

  • Fewer priorities: five or six real ones, no more — and your own list shorter still
  • Now, Soon, and Never: the sort that ends the argument about whether you forgot something
  • The urgency test: what happens if we don’t do this? Most urgencies, ignored for a week, turn out to have been noise
  • The price of focus: deliberately missing things — better to deliver three than track fifty
  • Never say no, show the trade — including to the principal, and the weekly review that keeps it honest

Maya: So what's the discipline? How do you actually protect it?

Marcus: Fewer priorities. If everything matters, nothing does. I keep the principal to five or six real priorities. No more. And my own list even shorter. Then three columns: now, soon, and never. Now means this week. Soon means this month. Never doesn't mean never. It means not now, written down so it stops buzzing in your head. The never list is a gift to your future self. It ends the argument about whether you forgot something. You didn't forget it. You decided.

Maya: Everything feels urgent, though. In the moment, how do you choose?

Marcus: One question: what happens if we don't do this? Not what's the benefit. What's the cost of not doing it. The answers sort themselves fast. Most urgencies, ignored for a week, turn out to have been noise. The real ones get louder.

Maya: What about the fear of missing out? Focus means ignoring things, and ignoring things feels dangerous in this role.

Marcus: It is dangerous. And that's the price. Here's the uncomfortable truth: focus means deliberately missing things. You have to make peace with that. The alternative is worse: noticing everything and finishing nothing. I'd rather miss ten things and deliver three than track fifty and deliver none. The chief of staff who tries to cover everything becomes wallpaper. The one who focuses becomes load-bearing.

Maya: Everyone says learn to say no. Nobody says how.

Marcus: Because no, said plainly, sounds like defiance. So don't say no. Say the trade. If we do this, here's what slips. That's not refusal, it's clarity. And protect the principal the same way. Half my job is saying no on their behalf, before the request reaches them. That's not obstruction, it's service. Every interruption you absorb is an hour of their attention saved for the work only they can do.

Maya: How do you say no to the principal themselves? That's the one that terrifies people.

Marcus: Same move. Never no, always the trade. And pick your moments. Don't fight for focus in the middle of a crisis. Build the habit in calm weeks: the short priority list, the weekly review where you ask what's still true. Then when the storm comes, the muscle is already there. The principal who has watched you protect focus for months will trust you when you say: this new thing costs us that old thing.

Maya: Is there a ritual? Something that keeps it honest week to week?

Marcus: The weekly review. Fifteen minutes, same time every week. Look at the priority list and ask: is this still true? What's changed? What did we finish, and did it matter? Kill one thing every week. Just one. A priority that died quietly, a meeting that outlived its purpose. Focus isn't built once. It's maintained, by subtraction.
