The loneliest seat in the building

Every company has exactly one chief of staff, which means no peers down the hall, no playbook to compare against, and work that is invisible by design. This episode names the tax — and the truth about recognition that decides who lasts in the role.

What you'll hear

  • The loneliness of being the only chief of staff in the company — no peers, no playbook, nothing to compare notes with
  • The invisible save: nine at night, three executives fighting over one slide, and a two-word text — handle it

Full transcript

Maya: Marcus, your own mother thought you were an assistant.

Marcus: She did. Dinner party, a few years ago. Someone asked what her son did for a living, and she said, with total confidence: he's an assistant to the CEO.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. Okay, I need the full story. Did you correct her?

Marcus: I stopped correcting her. Explaining what a chief of staff does at a dinner party takes longer than the dinner.

Maya: Did that sting? A little?

Marcus: Honestly, yes. Not because she used the wrong words. Because she was right about the visibility. Nobody sees this job. You can sit ten feet from the most powerful person in the company for five years, shape decisions worth millions, and at a dinner party you're still "an assistant to the CEO." That's the deal, and you have to make peace with it or it'll eat you.

Maya: That's funny, but it's also the whole reason this show exists, isn't it? Nobody knows what this job is.

Marcus: Nobody. And that's the loneliness of it. Most companies have exactly one chief of staff. One. No peers down the hall, nobody in the building quite understands your days. So you're out there wrestling with these invisible problems, and there's no one to compare notes with.

Maya: Give me one. An invisible problem. What does that actually look like?

Marcus: It's nine at night. The board deck is due at eight in the morning. Three executives are fighting over one slide, each convinced the other is misrepresenting their numbers. Your principal texts two words: handle it. So you get on the phone, you untangle it, the deck goes out clean. Nobody will ever know you were there. No one thanks you. The meeting goes well and the credit flows everywhere except to you. That is the job, on a Tuesday. And every chief of staff listening just nodded, because they've lived that exact night.

Maya: Your exact Tuesday. Okay, I felt that. But every job is lonely in its own way. What's actually different here?

Marcus: What's different is there's no playbook. There's no degree in chief of staff studies. Nobody grows up dreaming about this. People fall into it, from operations, from consulting, from being the executive assistant who kept getting promoted until someone said, congratulations, you're the chief of staff now.

Maya: Wait. That's actually how it happens?

Marcus: That's how it happens. And all the real knowledge, how to read your principal's mood in the first ten seconds of a meeting, how to tell your boss they're wrong without getting fired, all of it just lives in people's heads. Nobody writes it down. So this show is us trying to extract it, one conversation at a time.

Maya: Institutional knowledge for a profession that never wrote anything down. Okay, so that's for current chiefs of staff. What about people thinking about the job?

Marcus: They're the ones I worry about most. Right now there's almost no way to learn what this job actually is before you take it. The job descriptions are vague, the interviews are vague, and then you start and, surprise, it's nothing like you imagined. People wash out in six months. Not because they weren't good enough. Because nobody told them the truth first.

Maya: Give me one truth. The one nobody says out loud.

Marcus: Your wins belong to your principal. If you need public credit to feel successful.

Maya: This is the wrong job.

Marcus: You're already thinking like one. And here's why that breaks people: most high achievers run on recognition. It's fuel. Take the fuel away and the engine stops, usually around month eight, usually quietly. Nobody tells you that in the interview. But it's the difference between thriving and burning out in year one.

Maya: So recap: you're not alone, even when your own mother doesn't get it. Someone is finally writing the playbook down. The truth, told before you jump. And honestly, a voice for the invisible craft.

Marcus: Invisible doesn't have to mean silent. When this job is done brilliantly, the principal looks brilliant. That's the deal, most of us signed up happily. But naming what great looks like lifts the whole profession. Better chiefs of staff make better executives. Better executives build better companies. That's the chain.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. See you next week!
