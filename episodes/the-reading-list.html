Five books, five tools

Not a reading list for its own sake. Each of these five books survives because it hands a chief of staff one tool they can use the same week — on ownership, on leverage, on landing a new role, and on the discipline of less.

What you'll hear

  • Extreme Ownership (Jocko Willink & Leif Babin): own everything, especially when things break — ownership builds trust fast
  • Multipliers (Liz Wiseman): amplify everyone around you — ask daily whether you multiplied or diminished
  • The First 90 Days (Michael Watkins): learn, align, then act — secure early wins that matter to your principal
  • High Output Management (Andy Grove): leverage — your calendar is the job, so spend hours where leverage is highest
  • Essentialism (Greg McKeown): less but better — protect the principal's time and make tradeoffs visible instead of just saying no

Full transcript

Maya: Welcome back to Chief of Staff Confidential, the show where we help chiefs of staff get better at the job, one episode at a time. I'm Maya, and I'm here with Marcus. Marcus, today we're doing something a little different. No single topic. Instead, I want your reading list. If an aspiring chief of staff asked you which books actually matter, what would you hand them?

Marcus: I'd hand them five books. And I won't just name them. I'll give you the one takeaway from each that a chief of staff can use on Monday morning. Let's start with Extreme Ownership, by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. Two Navy SEAL officers, and the core idea is brutally simple. When anything goes wrong, the leader owns it. All of it. No blaming the team, no blaming circumstances, no blaming the fog of war.

Maya: That sounds intense for an office job. And a chief of staff usually doesn't even have formal authority over most of the people involved. How does that apply?

Marcus: That's exactly why it matters more, not less. A chief of staff carries enormous responsibility with very little formal power. When a launch slips, or a briefing doc isn't ready, or a meeting goes sideways, the easiest thing in the world is to point somewhere else. Extreme ownership means you say, that happened on my watch, and here's what I'm changing so it doesn't happen again. Principals notice that fast. It builds trust faster than almost anything else you can do.

Maya: So book one is really about what you do in the worst moment, not the best one.

Marcus: Exactly. Anyone can take credit. Ownership is proven when things break.

Maya: Okay, I'm sold. What's number two?

Marcus: Number two is Multipliers, by Liz Wiseman. She studied leaders across industries and found two types. Diminishers drain the intelligence out of a room. They always need to be the smartest person there, so everyone else gets smaller. Multipliers do the opposite. They amplify everyone around them. They ask the questions that make other people smarter.

Maya: That framing feels perfect for this role, because a chief of staff's entire job is making the principal and the team more effective.

Marcus: That's why it's on my list. The takeaway is a question you ask yourself at the end of every day. Did I multiply or diminish today? Did I make my principal sharper and my team stronger, or did I just make myself look useful? Here's a practical version. In your next meeting, instead of giving your opinion first, ask the quietest person in the room what they think. That's a multiplier move. It costs you nothing and it changes the room.

Maya: I like that. A daily question and one small habit. Book three?

Marcus: Book three is The First 90 Days, by Michael Watkins. This is for anyone stepping into the chief of staff role, or any big new role. Watkins' point is that transitions are when leaders fail most often, and it's rarely about competence. It's about not having a plan for the transition itself. He lays out how to learn fast, secure early wins, and build the relationships that matter before you actually need them.

Maya: So it's not, show up and be brilliant on day one.

Marcus: Right. It's, have a plan for day one through day ninety. Your first impression compounds, for better or worse. The chiefs of staff who struggle most are the ones who try to prove themselves before they understand the terrain. They reorganize the operating cadence in week two and wonder why everyone's cold. Watkins gives you the sequence. Learn first, align second, then act. And pick one or two early wins that matter to your principal, not to your ego.

Maya: Learn, align, act. That's going on a sticky note. What about the day to day grind, once you're settled in the seat?

Marcus: That's book four. High Output Management, by Andy Grove, the legendary Intel CEO. Grove's big idea is leverage. Every activity you do has leverage, meaning how much output it creates across the whole organization. A one on one with a struggling manager feels small in the moment. But if that conversation unblocks a team of twenty people, that's enormous leverage. Compare that to perfecting a slide deck that three people will glance at. A chief of staff lives in leverage. Your calendar is the job. Grove teaches you to audit your week and move your hours toward the highest leverage work.

Maya: I think a lot of chiefs of staff feel guilty about how much of their week disappears into meetings and one on ones. Grove would say that is the work.

Marcus: He would say exactly that. Managerial leverage is the work. The guilt comes from measuring yourself like an individual contributor. You're not one. Your output is the output of the people around you.

Maya: That's a reframe worth the price of the book alone. And the last one?

Marcus: Book five is Essentialism, by Greg McKeown. The disciplined pursuit of less. The takeaway for a chief of staff is about protecting the principal's time and your own. Every yes you give is a thousand future nos you don't get to say. The best chiefs of staff are ruthless, polite gatekeepers. Essentialism gives you the philosophy and the language for it. If it isn't a clear yes, it's a no. And here's the practical part. You don't say no by slamming the door. You say, here's what we'd have to drop to make room for that, is that the trade you want? Suddenly you're not the person who says no. You're the person who makes tradeoffs visible.

Maya: Okay, let me recap the shelf. Extreme Ownership: own everything, especially when things break. Multipliers: amplify everyone around you, and ask yourself daily whether you multiplied or diminished. The First 90 Days: plan your transition, learn, align, then act. High Output Management: your calendar is the job, spend your hours where the leverage is highest. And Essentialism: less but better, protect the calendar, and make tradeoffs visible instead of just saying no.

Marcus: That's the list. Five books, five habits. Read them in any order, but read them with a pen. The margin notes are where the real value is. And honestly, reread one of them every year. You'll be a different chief of staff each time, so you'll read a different book each time.

Maya: Great place to end. That's our episode, the reading list. If you pick up even one of these this month, we'd love to hear how it lands. I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. See you tomorrow.
