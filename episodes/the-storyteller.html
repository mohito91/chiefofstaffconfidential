Full transcript

Maya: Nobody remembers slide forty-seven.

Marcus: But everybody remembers a story.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. Today: storytelling. Which, I'll be honest, sounds soft for a show about getting things done.

Marcus: It's the opposite of soft. It's the hardest-working skill you have. Data informs people. Stories persuade them. You can put perfect numbers in front of a room and get nods. Tell the story behind the numbers and you get action. The chief of staff sits exactly where strategy meets humans, and stories are how you move humans.

Maya: Why does that work, though? What's actually happening in the room?

Marcus: Two things. Memory and emotion. Our brains are wired for narrative: a situation, a tension, a resolution. And decisions are emotional first, rational second. People decide with their gut and justify with data. A story speaks to the gut. That's why the same strategy, told as a story, gets funded, and told as a spreadsheet, gets filed.

Maya: Where do you actually use this? Give me the moments.

Marcus: The biggest one is the principal's narrative. Every CEO has a vision, but most can't tell it as a story. They tell it as a strategy. Your job is to help them shape it: where we were, what changed, where we're going, and why it matters. That's the spine of every great company story. When the CEO can tell that in three minutes, in a hallway, without slides, the whole organization starts moving in the same direction.

Maya: What about the board? That's numbers, not narratives.

Marcus: And that's why most board presentations are forgettable. The numbers matter, obviously. But wrap them in the story. We set out to do this. Here's what happened. Here's what we learned. Here's where we're going. The directors will remember the arc long after they forget the figures. And the figures land harder inside the arc, because now they mean something.

Maya: What if you're just not a natural storyteller? Some people freeze at "tell me a story."

Marcus: It's a craft, not a gift. Learn the spine: situation, tension, resolution. Practice on small stages before big ones. Tell the story of a project in a team meeting before you tell the company story at a town hall. And steal structure shamelessly. Every good story you've ever heard uses the same bones. The skill isn't inventing. It's selecting: which details earn their place, which ones get cut.

Maya: Hardest version. You have to tell a story the numbers don't support. A bad quarter. Layoffs. How do you do that honestly?

Marcus: You don't invent a better story. You tell the true one, with dignity. Here's what happened, here's what we're doing, here's what it means for you. People can handle hard truth. What they can't handle is feeling managed. Here's the uncomfortable truth: the fastest way to destroy trust is a cheerful story over a painful reality. Everybody in the room knows. So give them honesty, give them the plan, give them respect. That's the story that holds a team together in the bad times.

Maya: And the vision story? The future that isn't true yet. Where's the line between aspiration and fiction?

Marcus: Aspiration says: this is where we're going, and here's how. Fiction says: we're already there. The difference is the path. A vision story earns belief by showing the next three steps, not by pretending we've arrived. People will follow an honest climb. They'll resent a painted summit.

Maya: Let me recap. Data informs, stories persuade. Learn the spine: situation, tension, resolution. Help your principal tell the company story in three minutes, no slides. Wrap board numbers in narrative so they mean something. Tell hard truths with dignity, never cheerfully. And keep vision stories honest: show the climb, don't paint the summit.

Marcus: The chief of staff who can tell the story becomes the voice of the mission. And the voice of the mission is never out of work.

Maya: If this episode helped, follow the show and tap five stars — it's how other chiefs of staff find us.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. See you next week!
