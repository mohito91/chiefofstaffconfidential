The Change Agent

Monday, November 9, 2026  ·  5 minutes 34 seconds

A merger plan built by the book falls apart in week three, and the problem is not the plan. Marcus tells the story of the operations lead who quietly kept the old way alive, the conversation that finally moved him, what the textbooks never mention about resistance, and the rule he now runs every change by: keep two versions, and never run the clean one.

Why change management plans fail

The textbooks say: unfreeze, change, refreeze. Eight steps, start with urgency, build the coalition, never skip ahead. It all fits on one slide. And every real change ignores the slide completely — because the slide describes an organization, and organizations don't change. People do, one at a time, usually in private, and almost never in the order your plan needs.

What you'll hear

  • One merger, one story: the plan that was green on paper and dead underneath
  • Resistance as information — the operations lead defending twenty years of work, not blocking a plan
  • The sentence nobody had said, and the conversation that replaced the memo
  • Two versions of every change: the clean one for approval, the honest one you run — never run the clean slide
  • The reality test: the change is real when the old way stops being an option

Full transcript

Maya: The textbooks are lying to you.

Marcus: That's a big claim. Which textbooks?

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. The change management ones. Unfreeze, change, refreeze. Eight steps. Start with urgency, build the coalition, never skip ahead. It all fits on one slide. So why does every real change I've ever watched ignore the slide completely?

Marcus: Because the slide describes an organization. And organizations don't change. People do, one at a time, usually in private, and almost never in the order your plan needs. Let me tell you about the merger that taught me that.

Maya: Go on.

Marcus: Two divisions being folded into one. I had the plan, and the plan was beautiful. Timeline, milestones, owners, a steering committee, a comms calendar. The CEO announced it on a Monday. By Friday every box was green. And underneath the green, nothing had moved.

Maya: Nothing?

Marcus: The systems team was still approving work the old way. The sales teams were still keeping two sets of numbers, theirs and ours. And the whole thing ran through one man: the operations lead on the acquired side. Twenty years in that division. Every request I sent him came back answered, polite, complete, and quietly routed around. On paper he was complying. In practice the old division was still running, with him at the switch.

Maya: So what did you do first?

Marcus: The wrong thing. I sent a better memo. Clearer rationale, tighter deadlines, the CEO's name in the second paragraph. He replied within the hour. Very supportive. Changed nothing.

Maya: Of course.

Marcus: That's when I learned the first real lesson of the job. His resistance was never about the plan. You cannot fix a person problem with a document.

Maya: Then what was it about?

Marcus: I finally stopped sending things and went and sat in his office. No deck. I asked him what the merger looked like from his chair. And it came out in about four minutes. He had built the system we were replacing. Built it himself, over twenty years, fix by fix. Every message we'd sent about the new way said the same thing to him: your twenty years were the problem we're solving. He wasn't resisting the change. He was defending his life's work, and we'd never once said out loud that the old system was good. It had carried the company for two decades. It just couldn't carry what came next.

Maya: So the blocker was a sentence nobody had said.

Marcus: The blocker was a sentence nobody had said. Once it was said, by the CEO, in front of his team, the whole thing loosened in a week. He became the person who migrated his own people across. Nobody else could have done that. He had the trust. We just had the plan.

Maya: Hold on. You're describing a chief of staff's job as therapy with a timeline.

Marcus: I'm describing it as translation. The executives speak in outcomes. The floor speaks in consequences. Somebody has to stand in the middle and carry meaning both ways without losing it. The models never mention that person. In the textbooks the change just cascades. In real life there is no cascade. There's a person, walking it from room to room.

Maya: Okay, but be honest about the plan. You had milestones, a steering committee, the whole machine. Was any of it useful?

Marcus: All of it, as long as you know what it's for. I keep two versions of every change now. The clean one, for the board: the timeline, the milestones, the green boxes. And the honest one, that I actually run: the names, the worries, the three things most likely to break. The clean version gets you approval. The honest version gets you through. And the rule is simple. Never run the clean slide.

Maya: Never run it.

Marcus: Because the moment you start believing the tidy version, you go blind. You stop seeing the operations lead in his office until he's a crisis.

Maya: Where do the textbooks actually fail, then? Give me the one place.

Marcus: They assume resistance is a phase. Step three, manage resistance, move on. Resistance isn't a phase. It's information. It's the organization telling you what the change actually costs the people living it. The man in that office was the most honest signal I got in the whole merger, and I'd spent three weeks treating him as an obstacle.

Maya: How did you know it was finally real? Not announced. Real.

Marcus: Two months later we retired his old tracking system. The one he'd built. I expected a fight, or at least a funeral. Instead he ran the shutdown himself, and when someone asked for the old report the next week, he said, we don't do that one anymore. That's the test. The change is real when the old way stops being an option, and the person who loved it most is the one who closes the door.

Maya: So the textbooks describe change.

Marcus: And the chief of staff delivers it. Person by person, conversation by conversation, in rooms the plan never mentions.

Maya: If this episode helped, follow the show and tap five stars — it's how other chiefs of staff find us.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. See you next week!
