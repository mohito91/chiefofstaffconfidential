Own the Screen

Everyone obsesses over lighting and camera angles, but the person who owns the video call is the one who narrates it. Maya and Marcus dig into the single skill that makes you the most senior voice in any virtual room: saying what just happened, out loud, while it's happening. Full transcript

Maya: Last Tuesday I watched a VP with perfect lighting, perfect framing, perfect posture get completely ignored for an hour. And the person everyone listened to? Camera off for half the call.

Marcus: That's the whole game right there.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. Today: owning the screen. So what did the camera-off person do that the polished VP didn't?

Marcus: They narrated. Every twenty minutes or so, they'd come on and say what had just happened. "So what I'm hearing is we've agreed on the launch date, and Priya owns the pricing decision by Friday." Three sentences. And suddenly they're the most senior person on the call.

Maya: Without the title, without the lighting.

Marcus: Without anything visual at all. Here's what people get wrong about video: they think presence is the rectangle. The background, the lighting, the eye contact with the lens. All of that is fine, but it's stagecraft. What actually makes you powerful on a call is being the person who says what the meeting means, out loud, while it's happening.

Maya: Give me the mechanics. What does narrating actually sound like?

Marcus: Four moves. One: summarize the decision the moment it's made. Don't wait for the notes. "So we've decided X." Two: name the owner and the date, out loud. "Priya owns pricing by Friday." Three: state the alignment. "Let me make sure we're aligned — we're saying the launch moves to March, correct?" Four: use names constantly. "Priya, what's your read on this?" You just pulled someone in and showed the whole room you're paying attention.

Maya: Okay, but there's a failure mode here. The person who narrates everything becomes the person who won't shut up.

Marcus: Absolutely. And here's the line: narrate to clarify, never to perform. If you're summarizing to show how smart you are, everyone hears it. The test is simple — does your summary shorten the meeting or lengthen it? The good narrator ends discussions. "We've got what we need, let's move on." That's power.

Maya: What about getting into the conversation in the first place? On video you can't read the room for an opening. You just talk over someone.

Marcus: That's exactly where narration helps. Instead of jumping in with your opinion, jump in with a summary. "Can I check my understanding of what was just said?" Nobody experiences that as an interruption. It reads as service. You're not cutting someone off, you're carrying their point forward.

Maya: The tangent problem, though. Ten minutes in, your principal takes the whole meeting off course. The chief of staff usually runs these calls. What do you actually do?

Marcus: You name it gently and offer the way back. "That's worth its own conversation — can we park it and come back to the decision we need today?" The key word is decision. It reminds everyone why they're here without making it a confrontation. And do it early. The longer a tangent runs, the harder it is to kill. The person who rescues the agenda is the person running the room, title or not.

Maya: Here's my uncomfortable question. Isn't all the visual stuff — the lighting, the camera angle — just irrelevant then?

Marcus: Not irrelevant. Just overrated. Here's the uncomfortable truth: obsessing over your setup is procrastination that feels like preparation. The camera-off person proved it. Bad lighting never made anyone look junior. But you know what does? Silence. The people who feel invisible on video aren't invisible because of their webcam. They're invisible because they never say what the meeting decided.

Maya: What about the multitasking thing? Everyone does it. Camera on, eyes drifting to the second screen.

Marcus: People can always tell, and it destroys you faster than bad lighting ever could, because it says this meeting isn't worth my full attention. If you need to take notes, narrate it: "I'm going to take notes, so if I look down, that's why." Say it out loud and it becomes professionalism instead of distraction. See the pattern? The voice fixes everything.

Maya: So the whole episode is really one sentence: the person who says what happened owns what happens next.

Marcus: That's it. Summarize the decision, name the owner, state the alignment, use names. Do that consistently, and you'll own every room you're in. Even the virtual ones. Even with your camera off.

Maya: I'm Maya, he's Marcus, and this is Chief of Staff Confidential. See you next week!
